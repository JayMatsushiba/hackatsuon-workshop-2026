## 0. Two corrections to the brief before you write copy

- **"Hamaodori"** is not the name. The Minato Festival dance is **はまらいんや踊り = Hamarainya Odori** ("hamarainya" is local dialect, roughly "come and join us"). **[F/S]** Do not print "Hamaodori."
- **"Kadoware"** does not exist as far as I can find. Two likely intended things: (a) **角星 / Kakuboshi**, a sake brewery whose shop building is a nationally registered cultural property; (b) the **風待ち (Kazamachi, "waiting for the wind") district** restoration project. **[F]** Also, **"Uchiwatashi"** is almost certainly **内湾 / Naiwan** ("inner bay") — the Minamimachi/Uomachi waterfront district. **[F]** Use Naiwan.
- **"Kesennuma Crossing"** appears to be the **気仙沼湾横断橋 / Kesennuma Bay Crossing Bridge** (nickname かなえおおはし / Kanae Ōhashi), not a website or program. **[S]**

## 1. Identity as a distant-water (遠洋) fishing port

### Founding story and Edo period
- **1675**: a local figure recorded as **Kanemon** invited fishermen from **Wakayama (Kishū)** to teach **pole-and-line bonito fishing (一本釣り)**. This is treated on the official tourism site as the pivotal origin of Kesennuma's fishing identity. **[F]** — https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/oshietesensei_katsuo/ (page dated 2021-07-07)
- Knock-on Edo-period industries: dried/salted bonito processing; **sardine net fishing** to supply live bait; **sardine meal (干鰯) exported as fertilizer** to farming regions of western Japan; coastal shipping merchants carrying processed fish and **leaf tobacco to Edo**. **[F]** — https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/kesennuma_suisan_ryutsu/ (dated 2022-08-23)
- **1780s** travel accounts record visitors' astonishment at the bustling port, the "skillful fishing methods," and abundant cheap high-quality bonito. **[F]** (same katsuo page)
- **魚問屋 (uodonya)** fish wholesalers financed and provisioned boats from the Edo period; this later became a **ship-provisioning trade serving foreign fishing vessels**, building long-term relationships that sustained the port. **[F]**

### Meiji → Shōwa
- **Kamaboko (fish cake)** production began using **dogfish**, then switched to **shortfin mako shark** as dogfish were overfished — the seed of the "shark town" identity. **[F]**
- **Two major fires (1915 and 1926)** devastated the industry but accelerated modernization: motorized vessels, electrified processing. **[F]**
- **1919: Japan's first large-scale refrigeration facility** opened here, enabling year-round kamaboko distribution. **[F]** — flag as "first large-scale" per this source; worth a second source before printing "Japan's first."
- **1950s**: **saury (sanma)** landings peaked, canning boomed; Kesennuma was Japan's No.1 for sanma. **[F/S]**
- **1960 Chilean tsunami** destroyed aquaculture infrastructure — the rebuild is what enabled adoption of **wakame cultivation**. **[F]**
- **1960s onward**: bonito pole-and-line declined relative to **deep-sea tuna longlining**, which increased **shark bycatch** and revived shark processing. **[F]**

### Present-day records (all need re-verification — landing rankings change annually)
- **Fresh bonito (生鮮カツオ): No.1 in Japan.** Sources disagree on the streak: official tourism page (2021) says **24 consecutive years**; a JNTO/Tohoku-adjacent source says **25 consecutive years (2021, ~34,900 t)**; a 2024 search snippet says **27 consecutive years**. **[F for 24; S for 25/27]** Safest copy: "No.1 in Japan for fresh bonito landings for more than two decades running — check the current year's figure."
- **Shark and swordfish (メカジキ) landings: No.1 in Japan.** **[F/S]** The Shark Museum's own site claims Kesennuma accounts for **80%+ of Japan's domestic shark landings**. **[F]** — https://www.uminoichi.com/museum/
- Kesennuma sits beside the **Sanriku offshore grounds**, described in Japanese sources as one of the **world's three great fishing grounds**. **[S]** — https://journal.meti.go.jp/sanrikujoban/36070/

### Bonito culture vocabulary (good copy material, all [F])
- **のぼりガツオ / nobori-katsuo** — northbound spring/summer bonito.
- **もどりガツオ / modori-katsuo** — southbound autumn bonito, fattier.
- **バケ / bake** — barbless artificial lures developed locally so the fish body isn't damaged (originally because the catch went to katsuobushi flakes); this is cited as why Kesennuma bonito arrive in unusually good condition.
- **大漁旗 / tairyō-bata** — celebratory banners with bonito motifs; also used on garments given to crew.
- **大漁唄い込み / tairyō utaikomi** — harvest celebration songs. (Note: the fetched translation rendered this as "Tairyo Utagomikomi" — use 大漁唄い込み.)
- Small bonito figurines handed out at winter festivals as prayers for a big catch.
- The industry is framed as an ecosystem: markets, **ice suppliers**, box makers, **shipbuilders**, restaurants.

### Shark fin (フカヒレ)
- Kesennuma is a **leading shark fin producer**; processors' techniques refined through mutual competition. **[F]**
- **Whole-shark use**: fin, plus meat, cartilage, skin, and organs processed into products. **[F/S]**
- **むろね颪 / murone-oroshi** — a **northwesterly wind** in **early winter** that sun-dries the fins outdoors; described as a seasonal sight of the city. **[F]** — https://www.tohokukanko.jp/en/attractions/detail_1011317.html (site footer references FY2018 subsidy; content undated — treat as somewhat old but still accurate on the wind)
- Locally shark fin is eaten far beyond soup: **sushi, donburi, ramen**. **[F]** Kesennuma tourism site maintains a shark-fin menu roundup: https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/hukahiremenu2025/ (2025-05-09) **[F, list only]**
- **もうかの星 / mōka no hoshi** = raw **shark heart** (shortfin mako), a local delicacy. **[F, listed]** — https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/chinmi_moukanohoshi/ (2024-10-02), https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/mouka_no_hoshi/
- **Sensitivity note for an English site:** shark fin is ethically contested among Western readers. The local framing is *full utilization of bycatch from tuna/swordfish longlining*, not finning. If you cover it, use that framing and say so explicitly.

### Shipbuilding
- Kesennuma has built ships for **over 100 years**. **[S]**
- **みらい造船 / Mirai Shipbuilding**: formed **2015** by merging/consolidating **five** tsunami-damaged local shipyards; new yard completed **May 2019**; capacity cited as **10 large fishing vessels simultaneously, ~4 new builds/year**; installed a **ship lift — one of only three in Japan (2019)**. **[S]** — https://miraiships.co.jp/business/ , https://www.nikkei.com/article/DGXMZO49573200Z00C19A9L01000/ — **verify all these numbers directly; I only saw them in snippets.**

### Fish market as a visitor site
- **気仙沼市魚市場 / Kesennuma City Fish Market**: **2nd-floor viewing deck described as one of the largest in Japan**; watch bonito landings and auctions. Landings include bonito, tuna, billfish, saury, sharks. Also a **水産情報等発信施設** (fisheries information/experience facility) inside with real gear and large-screen video. **[S]** Official site: https://kesennuma-uoichiba.jp/ and tour route page https://kesennuma-uoichiba.jp/tourroute/
- Snippet figures **to verify**: open year-round **6:30–17:00**, **free** (paid if using a guide), best **6:00–8:00**; tel 0226-22-7119. **[S]** japan-guide gives "viewing 6am–8am, free." **[F]** — https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e5036_fish.html and https://www.japan-guide.com/e/e5036.html (last updated **2025-02-20** **[F]**)
- Official tourism site runs a **morning fish-market guided walk**: https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/fishmarket-morningtour/ **[S]**

## 2. The 2011 disaster and how the town tells the story

### ⚠️ Casualty figures — use these, and cite them
Kesennuma City's own damage page (data **as of 2023-08-31**, page updated **2023-09-12**) **[F]** — https://www.kesennuma.miyagi.jp/sec/s009/020/020/020/1300452011135.html
- **On a resident-registration basis: 1,356 total — 1,033 direct deaths, 111 disaster-related deaths, 212 missing.**
- **On a disaster-area basis: 1,434 total — 1,109 direct deaths, 111 disaster-related deaths, 214 missing.**
- **15,815 residences affected** (as of 2014-03-31); **~9,500 households affected** (estimate, 2011-04-27).

Other figures I saw, which **conflict** and should NOT be mixed with the above:
- The Memorial Museum's own English site states **1,143 deaths (including disaster-related deaths) and 212 still missing** in Kesennuma. **[F]** — https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/english/
- nippon.com (2021-03-09) says **1,246 killed**, city population **74,000 (Feb 2011) → 61,000 (Jan 2021)**. **[F]** — https://www.nippon.com/en/in-depth/d00685/

**Recommendation:** quote one source and attribute it. The cleanest is the city's resident-registration figure with the "as of Aug 2023" date, or the museum's own wording if you are writing about the museum. Do not average, round, or dramatize.

### The city's framing: 「海と生きる」 "Live with the Sea"
The subtitle of the **気仙沼市震災復興計画 (Kesennuma City Earthquake Reconstruction Plan)** is **「海と生きる」** — an explicit statement that a town repeatedly hit by tsunami has chosen to keep living with the sea rather than turn away from it. **[F/S]** — https://pride.kesennuma-kanko.jp/ocean/ , https://recovery.preventionweb.net/ja/node/90555 . This is the single most useful frame for English copy: it is the town's own words, it is not sentimental, and it explains the physical landscape (seawalls + deliberate sightlines to the water).

### 2a. 気仙沼市東日本大震災遺構・伝承館 — Ruins of the Great East Japan Earthquake, Kesennuma City Memorial Museum
Official site (Japanese + English), **actively maintained** **[F]** — https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/ , English: https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/english/ , facility info: https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/english/about/
(Note: `https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/en/` is a **404** — use `/english/`.)

**What it is:** the **former Miyagi Prefectural Kesennuma Koyo High School (宮城県気仙沼向洋高等学校旧校舎)**, preserved in the state the tsunami left it, plus a purpose-built 伝承館 (memorial/transmission hall) that serves as the entrance. **Opened March 2019.** **[F/S]**

**What a visitor actually sees [F]:**
- Enter through the new building; **video theatre with a ~300-inch screen** (japan-guide: "a sobering 20-minute video about the events of March 11"), before/after footage, plus **Exhibition Rooms A and B** on the disaster and on Kesennuma today.
- Then walk through the **preserved school building**: corridors and classrooms left as found, before/after photo panels, and — the signature sight — **an entire car deposited inside a third-floor classroom** by the water. **[F, japan-guide]**
- **Vehicles swept up by the tsunami** on site; the **former gymnasium ruins** (exterior viewing only); the **rooftop** where teachers escaped. **[F]**
- **Lecture room**, rentable training/workshop rooms, outdoor plaza (open sunrise–sunset, 365 days: playground equipment, dog run, benches). **[F]**
- **Typical visit 60–90 minutes.** **[F]**
- **Photography permitted only in the Disaster Ruins section.** **[F]** — important for a tourism site to state.
- **Mobile explanations in English, Chinese, Korean and Indonesian.** Free wheelchair rental; free lockers. **[F]**

**Inundation height — conflicting numbers, verify:** the official Kesennuma tourism site says the building was **damaged up to the 4th floor**; VISIT KESENNUMA's English page says **the tsunami reached 12 m, flooding up to the 3rd floor**. **[F both]** Do not print either without confirming with the museum.

**Prices/hours (as fetched — flag "verify before publishing") [F]:**

- Closed **Mondays** (open if a public holiday, closed the following day); year-end/new-year (official EN page says **Dec 29–Jan 4**; japan-guide says Dec 30–Jan 4 — verify).
- **Always open on commemorative days: the 11th of each month, 1 September (Disaster Prevention Day), 5 November (World Tsunami Awareness Day).** **[F]** Nice, concrete, and meaningful detail.
- Admission: **adults ¥600 / high school ¥400 / elementary & junior high ¥300**; groups of 30+ ¥500/¥300/¥200; annual passes ¥1,800/¥1,200/¥900; preschoolers free; 50% off for disability certificate holders + one companion.

**Bookable programs (reservation required) [F]** — https://www.kesennuma-memorial.jp/request/option.php

It says the design lets visitors "experience through their senses of sight, hearing and others what it means to face a natural disaster." **[F]** VISIT KESENNUMA calls it "a humbling experience." **[F]**

### 2b. リアス・アーク美術館 — Rias Ark Museum of Art (permanent disaster exhibit)
Official site **[F]**, actively maintained (news dated through **July 2026**), has an English toggle — https://rias-ark.sakura.ne.jp/2/ ; exhibit page https://rias-ark.sakura.ne.jp/2/1fgallery/

**「東日本大震災の記録と津波の災害史」 ("Records of the Great East Japan Earthquake and the History of Tsunami Disasters")** — **permanent, 1st floor, opened 3 April 2013.** **[F]**
- Curators conducted their **own two-year field documentation** of Kesennuma City and Minami-Sanriku Town after the disaster, accumulating **~30,000 photographs** and **~250 collected objects**. **[F]**
- **On display: 203 photographs, 155 被災物 (disaster-affected objects), 137 historical documents/records.** **[F]** These are unusually precise and worth quoting.
- Structure: **first half** = "report from the disaster sites"; **second half** = four thematic areas — survivors' feelings; what was lost (things and daily routine); preparing for the future; the town's history and its causal relationship to the damage. **[F]**
- Method/philosophy: curators wrote **first-person captions describing what they felt while documenting**; the 被災物 are split into objects that show **physical destructive force** and objects that stand for **stolen everyday life**, each with an interpretive narrative treating the object as a **story**. The museum acknowledges the exhibition is **text-heavy and demanding** and asks visitors to sit with it. Its stated position: "our assigned role is not merely to preserve records, but to transmit them correctly." **[F]**
- **Historical context**: Sanriku's earlier tsunami disasters are exhibited alongside 2011. **[F]**
- ⚠️ **Widely reported but not confirmed by me:** that the museum deliberately refuses the word **瓦礫/gareki ("rubble/debris")** because those objects were people's belongings. I asked for this and the fetched page did not confirm it. **[U]** — do not print without checking. (A scholarly PDF by the curator Yamauchi Hiroyasu likely covers it: https://f-gakkai.net/wp-content/uploads/2020/09/13-1-3YAMAUCHI.pdf — unfetched.)
- The permanent disaster exhibition has also **toured other cities** (e.g. Nagoya, and a Meguro Museum of Art show "気仙沼と、東日本大震災の記憶"). **[S]** — https://www.city.nagoya.jp/bousaiportal/shientorikumi/1013612.html , https://bijutsutecho.com/magazine/interview/293
- Registered by MLIT Tohoku as an official **震災伝承施設 (disaster memory transmission facility)**. **[F]** — https://www.thr.mlit.go.jp/shinsaidensho/facility/miyagi-3-008.html

### 2c. 気仙沼市復興祈念公園 — Kesennuma City Reconstruction Memorial Park
- **Opened 11 March 2021**, on **陣山 (Jinyama)** at the foot of **Mt. Anba (安波山)**, close to the city centre. **[S]**
- Centrepiece **「祈りの帆(セイル)」 "Sail of Prayer"**: **10 m tall**, made of the **aluminium alloy used for ship hulls**, shaped as both **a ship's sail and two hands joined in prayer**. **[S]**
- **Name plaques of the victims** arranged in a **circle, each panel facing the district where that person lived**, inscribed with **each individual's name and age at death**. Open to anyone, any time, to pay respects. **[S]**
- Sources: https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/kesennumapark20210311/ , https://www.tohokukanko.jp/attractions/detail_1009234.html — **[S]**, fetch before publishing.
- **Sensitivity:** this is an active place of mourning with victims' names. Copy should say so and advise quiet, respectful behaviour; avoid "photo spot" language.

### 2d. 命のらせん階段 — "Spiral Staircase of Life" (Abechō Shōten founder's former residence)
- Private disaster-heritage site in the **脇 (Waki)** district, registered as a 震災伝承施設. **[S]**
- The company chairman had the **external spiral staircase built four years before 2011**, having drawn lessons from the **1960 Chilean tsunami**, specifically so neighbours in a low-lying area far from high ground could reach the roof. Evacuation drills had been held with the community. **[S]**
- ⚠️ **Number of survivors conflicts**: a Kahoku Shimpo–derived snippet says **about 20 people**; the Kesennuma tourism model-course page (translated) says **about 30 people**. **[S vs F-translation]** Do not print a figure without confirming.
- Access appears to be **via Kesennuma Plaza Hotel's guided bus tour for guests**, not open walk-up. **[F, from model course]** Verify.
- Sources: https://kahoku.news/articles/20210919khn000013.html , https://www.reconstruction.go.jp/jireishuu/2022jirei/08/

- **気仙沼震災復興語り部ガイド** (Kesennuma reconstruction storyteller guides): began **the autumn after the disaster (2012)**; now speak about both the disaster and the town's recovery. **[S]** — https://www.tohokukanko.jp/attractions/detail_1010450.html
- **一般社団法人ボランティアステーションin気仙沼** offers kataribe guided tours. **[S]** — https://vsk311.com/guide

### Tone guidance for section 2 (my recommendation)
The town's own material is plain-spoken, specific, and pedagogical — not tragic. It leads with **numbers, objects, and lessons**, and pairs the disaster with **continuity** ("live with the sea"). Match that. Concretely: give figures with an "as of" date and a source; describe what is physically present rather than imagining feelings; name the kataribe programmes as the way to hear personal accounts (rather than retelling personal stories yourself); state the photography restriction at the Memorial Museum; note that the Memorial Park carries victims' names. Avoid: "ghost town," "wall of water," invented survivor anecdotes, drone-shot spectacle language.

### Gold-mining heritage — Japan Heritage 「みちのくGOLD浪漫」

### Temples and shrines
- **補陀寺 / Hodaji** (Sōtō Zen; mountain name 白樺山; principal image Nyoirin Kannon; **No. 30 of the Ōshū 33 Kannon pilgrimage**). Temple tradition dates founding to **890 (Kanpyō 2)** as a Tendai temple named 補陀落寺. Its **六角堂 (hexagonal hall)** was **built 1762 (Hōreki 12)**, **repaired 1802 (Kyōwa 2)**: sides **2.69 m**, height **8.26 m**, hexagonal pyramidal **red-tiled** roof, **vermilion-lacquered inside and out**, with **ink paintings on the boarded ceiling**. **Miyagi Prefecture–designated tangible cultural property (1969).** **[S/F]** — https://www.pref.miyagi.jp/soshiki/bunkazai/14hodaji.html , https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/補陀寺（六角堂）/
- **観音寺 / Kannonji** (Tendai; mountain name 海岸山; principal image Shō-Kannon; **No. 25 of the Tōhoku 36 Fudō pilgrimage**). Tradition: origins in the **Wadō era (708–714)**; the Kannon image carved by **Gyōki**; the halls organised and the temple renamed by **Jikaku Daishi Ennin in 850 (Kashō 3)**. Its **厨子 (altar shrine / zushi)** is prefecture-designated (1969). **[S]** — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/観音寺_(気仙沼市)
- **五十鈴神社 / Isuzu Jinja** (魚町 / Uomachi, on 神明崎 Shinmeizaki, jutting into the inner bay — the visible shrine promontory in the Naiwan townscape). Village-shrine rank; annual festival on the **11th day of the 9th lunar month**; traced to the **Ōei era (1394–1428)** of the Muromachi period, with its subsidiary **龍神社** possibly of far older origin (linked to a shrine listed in the **Engishiki, 927**). **[S]** — https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/五十鈴神社_(気仙沼市) , https://www.miyatabi.net/miya/kesenuma/isosuzu.html
- **早馬神社 / Hayama Jinja** (唐桑町宿 Karakuwa-chō Shuku). **Founded 1217**, by **梶原景実 (Kajiwara Kagezane)**, brother of Kamakura retainer **Kajiwara Kagetoki**. Known for **safe-childbirth** blessings and, being a "horse" shrine, has special significance in **Year of the Horse**. Its **autumn festival, first Sunday of October**, features a **mikoshi procession, a boat festival (船まつり), and folk performing arts**, drawing large crowds. **[S]** — https://hayama.jinja.jp/ (official), https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/hayamajinjya/
- **御崎神社 / Osaki Jinja** (tip of the Karakuwa Peninsula). **Over a thousand years** of history; guardian deity of **fishing and safe voyage**; affectionately called **「おさきさん」** by seafarers; also associated with **matchmaking (縁結び)**. **[S]** — https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/osakijinjya/ , https://genbu.net/data/mutu/osaki_title.htm , https://rekishijisya.com/osaki/
- **紫神社 / Murasaki Jinja** — the evacuation hill shrine behind the Minamimachi shopping street (§3). **[S]**
- **羽田神社 / Hata Jinja** — site of the nationally designated 羽田のお山がけ (§4). **[S]** — http://www.maroon.dti.ne.jp/hata-jj/ , https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/羽田神社/
- **みちびき地蔵 / Michibiki Jizō** (Kesennuma Ōshima) — a Jizō with a tradition of **guiding the dead to the Pure Land**, and a folk tale in which the Jizō warned a mother and child on Ōshima of an oncoming great tsunami: on the **Boys' Day (端午の節句)** eve a boy named **Hamakichi** and his mother encounter countless village spirits coming to greet the Jizō; the next day a huge tsunami strikes, and those who had come to greet the Jizō were the people who died in it. **Recorded as enshrined in the 1770s**; hall rebuilt in the Shōwa era; the tale was broadcast on **『まんが日本昔ばなし』 on 29 October 1977 (episode 107)** and circulated widely online again in **April 2011**. **[S]** — https://kesennuma-kanko.jp/みちびき地蔵/ , https://ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/みちびき地蔵 , https://www.j-cast.com/2011/04/27094429.html — **this is a strong, culturally-grounded piece for an English site, but handle carefully: it is folklore about tsunami death, and pairing it with 2011 needs a light touch.**

### Nationally designated intangible folk cultural properties (国指定重要無形民俗文化財) — 2, both listed under Kesennuma **[F]**
1. **羽田のお山がけ / Hata no Oyamagake** — designated **27 December 2000**. **Seven-year-old boys**, accompanied by **adult male relatives (not parents)**, climb **羽山 (Mt. Hane/Hata)** behind the Hata district to **月山神社 (Tsukiyama Shrine)** at the summit and back, praying for safe growth — a rite of passage marking a child's first entry into the shrine community, historically marking the Edo-period transition from infancy to boyhood, and deliberately excluding parents to break childhood dependence. Said to have continued **~400 years since the Edo period**. **Publicly performed on the 15th and 16th days of the 8th lunar month** (i.e. roughly **September**, varies yearly). Participants come from across the city including the former **Karakuwa** and **Motoyoshi** towns. **[S]** — https://www.pref.miyagi.jp/soshiki/bunkazai/02hata.html , https://bunka.nii.ac.jp/heritages/detail/137257 , https://matsurito.jp/matsuri/hatanoyamagake/index.html
   - ⚠️ Note the mountain/reading: sources give both 羽田 (place) and 羽山/羽田山 (mountain). Verify the reading (Hata vs Hada) and the mountain name.
2. **室根神社祭のマツリバ行事 / Murone Shrine Festival "Matsuriba" rites** — designated **1985**. The shrine is on **Mt. Murone in Ichinoseki, Iwate**, but the festival is **jointly listed with Kesennuma** because of its origin: the **Kumano deity was brought by sea from Kishū and first came ashore at Karakuwa in Kesennuma** before being enshrined on Mt. Murone (**718**, ~1,300 years of history). **On the festival's first day a 「御潮汲み」 (salt/seawater-drawing) rite is performed in Karakuwa Bay.** Held over **three days, 17–19 of the 9th lunar month, in the year following a leap year** (i.e. **not annual** — roughly every four years). Known as one of the wild festivals of Ōshū; features **two mikoshi**, plus fan dances, bell dances, and horse processions on the 「マツリバ」 ground. **[S]** — https://www.pref.miyagi.jp/soshiki/bunkazai/01murone.html , https://muronetaisai.com/about/ , https://www.city.ichinoseki.iwate.jp/index.cfm/6,3141,107,html
   - **This is a high-value story for an English site** (deity arrives by sea at Karakuwa; salt drawn from Karakuwa Bay each festival) but **verify the cycle and the next occurrence** — it is easy to get wrong.

### 虎舞 / Toramai (tiger dance) — the best "living tradition" story
- **Seven distinct toramai traditions survive within Kesennuma City — the most toramai-rich area in Miyagi Prefecture.** **[S]**
- The shared rationale: the proverb **「虎は千里往って千里還る」** ("the tiger goes a thousand ri and returns a thousand ri") — so the dance is offered as a **prayer for safe voyage and a big catch**, i.e. for fishermen to come home. **[S]** This is the single most quotable line in this whole report.
- **松圃虎舞 (Matsubatake Toramai)**: performed to fast-tempo **flute and large/small drums (打囃子)**; the **虎囃子** leader and a **two-person tiger** climb a **12-metre ladder** and dance on it. Offered at the **例祭 of 御崎神社**, the district's tutelary shrine, praying for safe voyage and abundant catch. **[S]**
- **浪板虎舞 (Namiita Toramai)**: origins uncertain, but said to have been offered **since the Edo period** at **飯綱神社 (Iizuna Jinja)** in Namiita, Kesennuma, for safe voyage. **Performed at Expo 2025 Osaka-Kansai — and had also performed at the 1970 Osaka Expo 55 years earlier.** **[S]** Great hook. — https://news.goo.ne.jp/article/oxtv/region/oxtv-26506.html , http://sanfes.com/wordpress2016/performers/1049.html
- A local NPO (まるオフィス) has run **Matsubatake Toramai experience programmes** in Karakuwa framed as "traditional performing arts that support Karakuwa's fisheries." **[S]** — https://maru-office.com/2018/03/matsubataketoramai-zemi_entry/ — **an experience like this would be strong tourism content; verify whether it currently runs.**
